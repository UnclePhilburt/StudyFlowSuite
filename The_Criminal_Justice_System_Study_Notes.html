The Criminal Justice System

Introductory Criminal Justice -- Comprehensive Study Notes

1. Overview and Purpose

Criminal Justice System: The set of government agencies and institutions responsible for enforcing law, adjudicating crime, and administering punishment. It operates through three interconnected components: law enforcement, the court system, and corrections.

Goals of the Criminal Justice System

  • Deterrence -- Discouraging criminal behavior through the threat of punishment (general and specific)
  • Incapacitation -- Removing offenders from society to prevent further crime
  • Rehabilitation -- Reforming offenders so they can reenter society as law-abiding citizens
  • Retribution -- Punishing offenders because they deserve it; "just deserts"
  • Restoration -- Repairing harm done to victims and the community through restorative justice practices
Two Competing Models (Herbert Packer, 1968):
Crime Control Model -- Prioritizes efficiency, swift punishment, and public safety. Emphasizes police/prosecutorial power.
Due Process Model -- Prioritizes individual rights, procedural fairness, and protection from government overreach. Emphasizes constitutional safeguards.

2. Law Enforcement

Structure of Policing in the U.S.

  • Federal -- FBI, DEA, ATF, U.S. Marshals, Secret Service, CBP, ICE
  • State -- State police / highway patrol; statewide jurisdiction
  • Local -- Municipal police departments and county sheriff's offices (largest proportion of officers)
  • Special jurisdiction -- Campus police, transit police, tribal police

Key Policing Strategies

StrategyDescription
Preventive PatrolOfficers patrol designated areas to deter crime through visible presence
Community PolicingBuilding partnerships between police and communities; emphasis on trust and problem-solving
Problem-Oriented Policing (POP)Identifying and addressing underlying causes of recurring crime problems (SARA model: Scan, Analyze, Respond, Assess)
Broken Windows TheoryAddressing minor disorders (vandalism, loitering) to prevent escalation to serious crime (Wilson & Kelling, 1982)
Hot Spots PolicingConcentrating resources in geographic areas with high crime rates
CompStatData-driven management tool using crime statistics for resource allocation and accountability

Constitutional Limits on Police Power

  • 4th Amendment -- Protection against unreasonable search and seizure; requires probable cause for warrants
  • 5th Amendment -- Right against self-incrimination; due process clause
  • 6th Amendment -- Right to counsel, speedy trial, confrontation of witnesses
  • 14th Amendment -- Equal protection; applies Bill of Rights to state governments
Exclusionary Rule (Mapp v. Ohio, 1961): Evidence obtained through unconstitutional means is inadmissible in court. Designed to deter police misconduct.
Miranda v. Arizona (1966): Suspects must be informed of their rights (right to remain silent, right to an attorney) before custodial interrogation. Failure to "Mirandize" can result in suppressed statements.
Terry v. Ohio (1968): Officers may conduct a brief "stop and frisk" if they have reasonable suspicion that a person is involved in criminal activity and may be armed.

3. The Court System

Dual Court Structure

Federal Courts

  • U.S. District Courts (94 trial courts)
  • U.S. Courts of Appeals (13 circuits)
  • U.S. Supreme Court (final authority)
  • Handle federal crimes, constitutional issues, cases between states

State Courts

  • Trial courts (general and limited jurisdiction)
  • Appellate courts (intermediate)
  • State supreme courts
  • Handle the vast majority (~95%) of all criminal cases

Key Courtroom Actors

Criminal Case Process

  • Arrest -- Based on probable cause; booking and processing
  • Initial Appearance -- Informed of charges; bail/bond determination
  • Preliminary Hearing / Grand Jury -- Determines if sufficient evidence exists to proceed (probable cause standard)
  • Arraignment -- Formal reading of charges; defendant enters plea (guilty, not guilty, nolo contendere)
  • Pretrial Motions -- Motion to suppress, motion to dismiss, discovery
  • Plea Bargaining -- Negotiation between prosecution and defense; resolves approximately 90-95% of criminal cases
  • Trial -- Jury selection (voir dire), opening statements, evidence presentation, closing arguments, jury deliberation, verdict
  • Sentencing -- Determined by judge (sometimes jury); guided by statutory ranges, sentencing guidelines, and presentence investigation reports
  • Appeal -- Review of legal errors, not facts; does not constitute double jeopardy
Burden of Proof: The prosecution must prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt -- the highest standard of proof in the legal system. The defendant is presumed innocent until proven guilty.

4. Sentencing

Types of Sentences

  • Determinate Sentencing -- Fixed term set by statute (e.g., 5 years); offender serves the specified time
  • Indeterminate Sentencing -- Range set by judge (e.g., 5-10 years); parole board determines release
  • Mandatory Minimums -- Legislatively required minimum prison terms for certain offenses (e.g., drug trafficking, firearms offenses)
  • Three Strikes Laws -- Enhanced sentences (often life) for repeat felony offenders
  • Truth-in-Sentencing -- Requires offenders to serve a substantial portion (typically 85%) of their sentence

Sentencing Disparities

  • Research consistently shows racial and socioeconomic disparities in sentencing outcomes
  • Federal Sentencing Guidelines (1987) attempted to reduce disparity but made advisory after United States v. Booker (2005)
  • The Fair Sentencing Act (2010) reduced the crack-to-powder cocaine sentencing ratio from 100:1 to 18:1

5. Corrections

Community Corrections (Non-Institutional)

  • Probation -- Court-ordered supervision in the community in lieu of incarceration; most common form of correctional supervision in the U.S.
  • Parole -- Supervised early release from prison; offender must comply with conditions or face revocation
  • Diversion Programs -- Alternative to prosecution (drug courts, mental health courts, veterans courts)
  • Electronic Monitoring -- GPS tracking or home confinement as an alternative to incarceration
  • Restorative Justice -- Victim-offender mediation, community service, restitution

Mass Incarceration

  • The U.S. has the highest incarceration rate in the world (~1.9 million incarcerated as of 2024)
  • Driven by the War on Drugs (1970s-present), mandatory minimums, three strikes laws, and truth-in-sentencing policies
  • Disproportionately affects Black and Hispanic communities
  • Reform movements include decriminalization, sentence reduction, and reentry programs
Reentry Challenges: Released offenders face barriers including employment discrimination, housing restrictions, loss of voting rights (in some states), limited access to public benefits, social stigma, and high recidivism rates (~44% rearrested within the first year).

6. Juvenile Justice

Parens Patriae: The doctrine that the state acts as a guardian for those who cannot protect themselves, including juveniles. This philosophy shaped the creation of a separate juvenile court system (first established in Cook County, Illinois, 1899).

Key Juvenile Justice Cases

  • In re Gault (1967) -- Juveniles have due process rights including right to counsel, right to notice of charges, right to confront witnesses, and privilege against self-incrimination
  • Roper v. Simmons (2005) -- Death penalty unconstitutional for offenders under 18
  • Graham v. Florida (2010) -- Life without parole unconstitutional for juveniles in non-homicide cases
  • Miller v. Alabama (2012) -- Mandatory life without parole unconstitutional for juveniles in all cases

7. Current Issues and Reform

  • Police Use of Force -- Debate over use-of-force policies, body cameras, qualified immunity, and accountability
  • Racial Disparities -- Overrepresentation of minorities at every stage of the system (stops, arrests, prosecution, sentencing, incarceration)
  • Wrongful Convictions -- Innocence Project data shows eyewitness misidentification and false confessions as leading causes; DNA exonerations
  • Bail Reform -- Movement to reduce or eliminate cash bail, which disproportionately harms low-income defendants
  • Drug Policy Reform -- Decriminalization and legalization trends (marijuana); shift toward treatment over incarceration
  • Mental Health -- Jails and prisons as de facto mental health facilities; crisis intervention teams (CIT); co-responder models
  • Technology -- Predictive policing, facial recognition, electronic surveillance; privacy and bias concerns
  • Prisoner Reentry -- Ban-the-box initiatives, Second Chance Act, transitional housing programs
