LITERARY DEVICES - ENGLISH STUDY GUIDE

Figurative Language:

Simile:
- Comparison using "like" or "as"
- Compares two unlike things
- Examples:
  * "Her smile was as bright as the sun"
  * "He runs like the wind"
  * "The clouds look like cotton candy"

Metaphor:
- Direct comparison without "like" or "as"
- States one thing IS another
- Examples:
  * "Time is money"
  * "Her eyes were daggers"
  * "The classroom was a zoo"

Extended Metaphor:
- Metaphor developed over multiple lines or throughout entire work
- Example: Life is a journey (with paths, destinations, obstacles)

Personification:
- Giving human qualities to non-human things
- Examples:
  * "The wind whispered through the trees"
  * "Time marches on"
  * "The flowers danced in the breeze"

Hyperbole:
- Extreme exaggeration
- Not meant to be taken literally
- Examples:
  * "I'm so hungry I could eat a horse"
  * "I've told you a million times"
  * "This bag weighs a ton"

Understatement:
- Deliberately representing something as less important
- Opposite of hyperbole
- Example: "It's just a scratch" (for a deep wound)

Idiom:
- Expression with non-literal meaning
- Culturally specific
- Examples:
  * "It's raining cats and dogs" (raining heavily)
  * "Break a leg" (good luck)
  * "Piece of cake" (very easy)

Alliteration:
- Repetition of initial consonant sounds
- Examples:
  * "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers"
  * "She sells seashells by the seashore"
  * "Big bad bear"

Assonance:
- Repetition of vowel sounds within words
- Examples:
  * "Try to light the fire"
  * "Go slow over the road"
  * "Hear the mellow wedding bells" (Poe)

Consonance:
- Repetition of consonant sounds within or at end of words
- Examples:
  * "Pitter patter"
  * "All mammals named Sam are clammy"

Onomatopoeia:
- Words that imitate sounds
- Examples:
  * Buzz, hiss, boom, crash
  * Meow, woof, moo
  * Sizzle, crack, pop

Oxymoron:
- Two contradictory words together
- Examples:
  * "Jumbo shrimp"
  * "Deafening silence"
  * "Act naturally"
  * "Living dead"

Paradox:
- Statement that seems contradictory but reveals truth
- Examples:
  * "Less is more"
  * "The beginning of the end"
  * "I can resist anything except temptation" (Oscar Wilde)

Poetry Devices:

Rhyme:
- Words with same ending sounds
- Types:
  * End rhyme: at ends of lines
  * Internal rhyme: within same line
  * Slant rhyme: similar but not exact sounds

Rhyme Scheme:
- Pattern of rhymes in poem
- Labeled with letters (ABAB, AABB, etc.)
- Example:
  Roses are red (A)
  Violets are blue (B)
  Sugar is sweet (C)
  And so are you (B)
  (Rhyme scheme: ABCB)

Meter:
- Rhythmic pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables
- Common meters:
  * Iambic: unstressed-STRESSED (da-DUM)
  * Trochaic: STRESSED-unstressed (DUM-da)
  * Anapestic: unstressed-unstressed-STRESSED
  * Dactylic: STRESSED-unstressed-unstressed

Iambic Pentameter:
- Five iambs per line
- Most common meter in English poetry
- Example: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" (Shakespeare)

Stanza:
- Group of lines forming unit
- Like paragraph in prose
- Types: couplet (2 lines), tercet (3), quatrain (4), etc.

Enjambment:
- Sentence continues from one line to next without punctuation
- Creates flow and suspense

Caesura:
- Pause in middle of line
- Often indicated by punctuation

Narrative Techniques:

Point of View:

First Person:
- Narrator is character in story
- Uses "I," "me," "we"
- Limited to narrator's knowledge
- Example: "I walked down the street"

Second Person:
- Narrator addresses reader as "you"
- Rare in literature
- Example: "You enter the room"

Third Person Limited:
- Narrator outside story
- Focuses on one character's thoughts
- Uses "he," "she," "they"

Third Person Omniscient:
- All-knowing narrator
- Can access all characters' thoughts
- Can see past, present, future

Unreliable Narrator:
- Narrator whose credibility is questioned
- May be lying, delusional, or naive

Foreshadowing:
- Hints about future events
- Creates suspense and anticipation
- Example: Dark clouds suggesting coming storm

Flashback:
- Scene from the past
- Interrupts chronological narrative
- Provides backstory or context

Irony:

Verbal Irony:
- Says opposite of what is meant
- Sarcasm is harsh form
- Example: Saying "Great weather!" during rainstorm

Situational Irony:
- Outcome opposite of what's expected
- Example: Fire station burns down

Dramatic Irony:
- Audience knows something characters don't
- Creates tension
- Example: In Romeo and Juliet, we know Juliet isn't dead

Symbolism:
- Object, character, or event represents abstract idea
- Examples:
  * Dove = peace
  * Red rose = love
  * Journey = life
  * Spring = rebirth

Motif:
- Recurring element with symbolic significance
- Pattern throughout work
- Example: Blood in Macbeth

Theme:
- Central idea or message
- Universal truth about life
- Examples: love, death, revenge, coming of age

Allegory:
- Story with two meanings: literal and symbolic
- Extended metaphor
- Example: Animal Farm (Orwell) represents Russian Revolution

Tone:
- Author's attitude toward subject
- Examples: serious, humorous, sarcastic, optimistic, pessimistic
- Conveyed through word choice and style

Mood:
- Emotional atmosphere of work
- How it makes reader feel
- Examples: suspenseful, gloomy, joyful, tense

Diction:
- Word choice
- Formal vs informal
- Simple vs complex
- Creates tone and mood

Syntax:
- Sentence structure
- Simple, compound, complex sentences
- Short vs long sentences
- Affects pacing and emphasis

Imagery:
- Descriptive language appealing to senses
- Visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory
- Creates vivid mental pictures
- Example: "The crimson sunset blazed across the horizon"

Allusion:
- Reference to historical event, literature, mythology, religion
- Assumes reader's knowledge
- Examples:
  * "He's a real Romeo" (references Shakespeare)
  * "This is her Achilles heel" (Greek mythology)
  * "Don't be a Scrooge" (Dickens)

Juxtaposition:
- Placing contrasting elements side by side
- Highlights differences
- Example: wealth next to poverty

Foil:
- Character who contrasts with another
- Highlights traits of main character
- Example: Mercutio as foil to Romeo

Protagonist:
- Main character
- Hero of story
- Reader usually sympathizes with

Antagonist:
- Opposes protagonist
- Creates conflict
- Not always a villain

Character Development:

Static Character:
- Doesn't change throughout story
- Same personality beginning to end

Dynamic Character:
- Changes significantly
- Grows or learns
- Most protagonists are dynamic

Round Character:
- Complex and realistic
- Multiple personality traits
- Like real people

Flat Character:
- Simple, one-dimensional
- Often minor characters
- Defined by one or two traits

Conflict:

Types of Conflict:
- Character vs. Character
- Character vs. Self (internal)
- Character vs. Society
- Character vs. Nature
- Character vs. Technology
- Character vs. Supernatural/Fate

Plot Structure:

Exposition:
- Introduction
- Setting, characters, background

Rising Action:
- Events building tension
- Complications arise

Climax:
- Turning point
- Moment of highest tension
- Most exciting part

Falling Action:
- Events after climax
- Consequences unfold

Resolution (Denouement):
- Conclusion
- Loose ends tied up
- Final outcome

Dialogue:
- Conversation between characters
- Reveals character, advances plot
- Can show conflict, relationships

Monologue:
- Long speech by one character
- Other characters present

Soliloquy:
- Character speaks thoughts aloud
- Alone on stage
- Reveals inner thoughts
- Common in Shakespeare

Aside:
- Comment to audience
- Other characters don't hear
- Used in plays
