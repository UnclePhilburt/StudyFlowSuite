CELL STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION - BIOLOGY STUDY GUIDE

Cell Theory:

Three Main Points:
1. All living things are composed of one or more cells
2. The cell is the basic unit of life
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells

History:
- Robert Hooke (1665): first observed cells in cork
- Anton van Leeuwenhoek: observed living cells
- Matthias Schleiden: all plants made of cells
- Theodor Schwann: all animals made of cells
- Rudolf Virchow: cells come from other cells

Types of Cells:

Prokaryotic Cells:
- No nucleus
- No membrane-bound organelles
- Smaller (1-10 micrometers)
- Simpler structure
- DNA in nucleoid region
- Examples: Bacteria, Archaea
- Evolved first (3.5 billion years ago)

Eukaryotic Cells:
- Have nucleus
- Have membrane-bound organelles
- Larger (10-100 micrometers)
- More complex
- DNA in nucleus
- Examples: Animals, Plants, Fungi, Protists
- Evolved later (2 billion years ago)

Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane):

Structure:
- Phospholipid bilayer
- Hydrophilic heads (water-loving) outside
- Hydrophobic tails (water-fearing) inside
- Embedded proteins
- Cholesterol molecules
- Carbohydrate chains

Fluid Mosaic Model:
- Membrane is flexible
- Components can move laterally
- Like fluid with floating proteins

Functions:
- Selectively permeable barrier
- Controls what enters/exits cell
- Cell recognition
- Cell communication
- Maintains homeostasis

Transport Across Membrane:

Passive Transport:
- No energy required
- Moves with concentration gradient (high to low)

Types of Passive Transport:

Diffusion:
- Movement of molecules from high to low concentration
- Example: Perfume spreading in room
- Continues until equilibrium

Osmosis:
- Diffusion of water across membrane
- Water moves to area of higher solute concentration
- Affects cell size

Facilitated Diffusion:
- Uses channel or carrier proteins
- Still passive (no energy)
- Example: Glucose entering cell

Active Transport:
- Requires energy (ATP)
- Moves against concentration gradient (low to high)
- Uses protein pumps
- Example: Sodium-potassium pump

Bulk Transport:
- Moving large particles or volumes

Endocytosis:
- Taking materials into cell
- Cell membrane engulfs substance
- Types:
  * Phagocytosis: "cell eating" (solids)
  * Pinocytosis: "cell drinking" (liquids)
  * Receptor-mediated: specific molecules

Exocytosis:
- Removing materials from cell
- Vesicle fuses with membrane
- Releases contents outside
- Example: Neurotransmitter release

Nucleus:

Structure:
- Largest organelle
- Surrounded by double membrane (nuclear envelope)
- Nuclear pores allow material exchange
- Contains chromatin (DNA + proteins)
- Nucleolus inside (makes ribosomes)

Functions:
- Stores genetic information (DNA)
- Controls cell activities
- Site of DNA replication
- Site of transcription (making RNA)

Chromatin vs Chromosomes:
- Chromatin: relaxed, spread out DNA
- Chromosomes: condensed DNA (during cell division)

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER):

Structure:
- Network of folded membranes
- Connected to nuclear envelope
- Two types: rough and smooth

Rough ER:
- Has ribosomes attached
- Looks bumpy
- Functions:
  * Protein synthesis
  * Protein modification
  * Protein transport

Smooth ER:
- No ribosomes
- Looks smooth
- Functions:
  * Lipid synthesis
  * Carbohydrate metabolism
  * Detoxification (liver cells)
  * Calcium storage (muscle cells)

Ribosomes:

Structure:
- Made of RNA and protein
- Two subunits (large and small)
- No membrane
- Found free in cytoplasm or attached to rough ER

Function:
- Protein synthesis
- Read mRNA and assemble amino acids
- "Protein factories"

Golgi Apparatus (Golgi Body):

Structure:
- Stack of flattened membrane sacs
- Like pancakes stacked
- Distinct cis face (receiving) and trans face (shipping)

Functions:
- Modifies proteins from ER
- Packages proteins into vesicles
- Adds carbohydrate tags
- Sorts and ships proteins
- "Post office" of cell

Lysosomes:

Structure:
- Membrane-bound sacs
- Contain digestive enzymes
- Acidic interior (pH 5)

Functions:
- Break down macromolecules
- Digest old organelles (autophagy)
- Digest engulfed materials
- "Recycling center" of cell
- More common in animal cells

Mitochondria:

Structure:
- Double membrane
- Outer membrane smooth
- Inner membrane folded (cristae)
- Matrix inside
- Have own DNA (circular)
- Can self-replicate

Functions:
- Cellular respiration
- Produce ATP (energy)
- "Powerhouse" of cell

Cellular Respiration:
- Glucose + Oxygen → CO2 + Water + ATP
- C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + 36-38 ATP

Endosymbiotic Theory:
- Mitochondria were once independent bacteria
- Engulfed by larger cell
- Evidence: own DNA, double membrane, reproduce independently

Chloroplasts (Plant Cells Only):

Structure:
- Double membrane
- Stacks of thylakoids (grana)
- Stroma (fluid)
- Have own DNA (circular)

Functions:
- Photosynthesis
- Convert light energy to chemical energy
- Produce glucose and oxygen
- Only in plant cells and some protists

Photosynthesis:
- 6CO2 + 6H2O + light → C6H12O6 + 6O2

Cytoskeleton:

Components:

Microfilaments:
- Thinnest (7 nm)
- Made of actin protein
- Functions:
  * Cell shape
  * Cell movement
  * Muscle contraction
  * Cell division (cleavage furrow)

Intermediate Filaments:
- Medium size (8-10 nm)
- Various proteins
- Functions:
  * Structural support
  * Anchor organelles
  * Maintain cell shape

Microtubules:
- Thickest (25 nm)
- Made of tubulin protein
- Functions:
  * Cell shape
  * Organelle movement
  * Chromosome movement in cell division
  * Form cilia and flagella
  * Tracks for motor proteins

Centrosomes and Centrioles:

Centrosome:
- Organizes microtubules
- Near nucleus
- Microtubule organizing center (MTOC)

Centrioles:
- Pair of cylindrical structures
- Made of microtubules
- In centrosome
- Important for cell division
- Form spindle fibers
- Present in animal cells, not plant cells

Cilia and Flagella:

Structure:
- Made of microtubules
- "9+2" arrangement
- Extend from cell surface
- Covered by plasma membrane

Cilia:
- Short and numerous
- Beat in coordinated waves
- Example: Respiratory tract cells
- Move materials across cell surface

Flagella:
- Long and few (usually one or two)
- Whip-like motion
- Example: Sperm cells
- Propel cell through liquid

Vacuoles:

Plant Cell Central Vacuole:
- Very large (up to 90% of cell volume)
- Filled with cell sap (water, ions, nutrients, wastes)
- Functions:
  * Storage
  * Maintains turgor pressure
  * Waste disposal
  * Cell growth

Animal Cell Vacuoles:
- Smaller and more numerous
- Temporary
- Food vacuoles
- Contractile vacuoles (remove excess water)

Cell Wall (Plant Cells, Bacteria, Fungi):

Structure:
- Outside plasma membrane
- Made of cellulose in plants
- Rigid and strong
- Porous (allows materials through)

Functions:
- Protection
- Structural support
- Prevents over-expansion from water uptake
- Maintains cell shape

Primary Cell Wall:
- Thin and flexible
- Allows growth
- All plant cells have

Secondary Cell Wall:
- Thicker and stronger
- Added after growth stops
- Some plant cells (wood)
- Contains lignin

Plasmodesmata (Plant Cells):
- Channels through cell walls
- Connect adjacent plant cells
- Allow communication and transport
- Like tiny tunnels between cells

Peroxisomes:

Structure:
- Membrane-bound organelle
- Contain enzymes

Functions:
- Break down fatty acids
- Detoxify harmful substances
- Produce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)
- Convert H2O2 to water (using catalase enzyme)

Comparing Plant and Animal Cells:

Both Have:
- Cell membrane
- Nucleus
- Ribosomes
- Endoplasmic reticulum
- Golgi apparatus
- Mitochondria
- Cytoskeleton

Only Plant Cells Have:
- Cell wall
- Chloroplasts
- Large central vacuole
- Plasmodesmata

Only Animal Cells Have:
- Centrioles
- Lysosomes (more common)
- Small vacuoles

Cell Size:

Why Cells Are Small:
- Surface area to volume ratio
- As cell gets bigger, volume increases faster than surface area
- Need large surface area for exchange of materials
- Larger cells less efficient
- Limits on how large cells can grow

Surface Area to Volume Ratio:
- Small cells: high ratio (efficient)
- Large cells: low ratio (inefficient)
- Explains why organisms have many small cells rather than few large cells

Levels of Organization:
1. Organelles
2. Cells
3. Tissues (groups of similar cells)
4. Organs (groups of tissues)
5. Organ systems (groups of organs)
6. Organism (complete individual)

Cell Specialization:
- Cells become specialized for specific functions
- Differentiation: process of becoming specialized
- Examples:
  * Nerve cells: long extensions for signaling
  * Red blood cells: no nucleus, carry oxygen
  * Muscle cells: many mitochondria for energy
  * Root hair cells: long projections for absorption
